History And Origin Of Egbema

Rooted in the Niger Delta, shaped by the flow of Oil and ancestral traditions

The Egbema kingdom, located in Imo and Rivers states, is an ancient, oil-rich, primarily Igbo-speaking community (specifically 16 villages) with deep roots in the Niger Delta. Historically linked to early Portuguese trade (c. 1445), it became a crucial trading hub, now heavily impacted by oil exploitation, pollution, and administrative division.

Egbema Oil Kingdom is an oil rich region dedicated to sustainable energy development, innovation, and community growth. With decades of experience, we excel in exploration, extraction, and energy management, ensuring a brighter future for our stakeholders and nation.

Origins And History

  • Migration and Location: The Egbema people in Imo/Rivers states have historical links to, or migrations from, older Delta regions, with traditions referring to early trade with Europeans. The kingdom was divided on Feb 3, 1976, leaving 13 villages in the Ohaji/Egbema LGA of Imo State and 3 in Ogba/Ndoni/Egbema LGA of Rivers State.
  • Trade and Commerce: Historically, Egbema served as a middleman in trade with European merchants in the 15th-17th centuries, supplying goods to the Bini region.
  • Cultural Identity: The Egbema are traditionally considered an Igbo-speaking community or a distinct ethnic group with cultural links to the Niger Delta, and distinguished themselves from the Ijaw Egbema group in Delta State.
  • Traditions: Traditions suggest origins from both the Benin Empire and Igbo heartland (Isuama area) around the 15th-16th century. They settled along the Orashi River, establishing a society deeply connected to water and agriculture, known as Alinso (holy land).
  • Conflict & Unity: Despite being politically split between two states (Imo and Rivers), Egbema people regard themselves as one, often referring to their united traditional leadership under an Eze.

How The Egbema People Came To Be

The Egbema people (often referred to as Egbema-Alinso) are an ethnic nationality with a strong Igbo cultural identity, primarily situated in the Ohaji/Egbema Local Government Area of Imo State and the Ogba-Egbema-Ndoni Local Government Area of Rivers State. While some Ijaw neighboring clans claim shared roots, the Egbema of Imo/Rivers are primarily an Igbo-speaking group with 16 traditional communities that share a common history and ancestry.

Origins And Settlement

Oral traditions suggest that the Egbema people originated from a migration from the ancient Benin Empire or the Igbo heartland (Isuama area) around the 15th-16th century.

The Progenitor: The community was founded by a progenitor named Egbema.

Settlement Site: Early settlers initially established themselves at a marshy location known as Ali Upa, located between present-day Okwuzi and Mgbede.

The First Separation: Due to annual flooding at Ali Upa, the community separated under two leaders (sometimes referred to as brothers): Ntia, who moved to found Mgbede, and Ofeke, who moved to found Okwuzi.

The 16 old Communities and Disintegration (Decentralization): Over time, these two original settlements expanded into a distinct clan structure of 16 communities, including Abacheke, Mmahu, Obiakpu, Etekwuru, Mgbede, Aggah, Okwuzi, Obokofia, Abaezi, Umudike, Umuorji, Mgbara, Opuoma, Obeakpu, Ekugba, and Ofuruola.

Other Expanded Communities in Egbema Are: Ebocha, Ekpe-Aggah, Ekpe-Mgbede, Ekpe-Etekwuru, Ekpe-Ekugba, Ekpe-Umuorji, Ekpe-Umudike, Ojiji, Ugada-Mmahu, Ugada-Abacheke, Ugada-Okwuzi,

Under Obiakpu Are: Ukwugba, Elua, Nwari, Aliocha 1, & 2, Nkpataku 1, Nkpataku Ishiugwu, Iyioji, Udogalezhi, Omanpe, Ajagwu, Anchor Inn, Nchichiogologo (Location),

The Split (1976): Originally, these 16 communities functioned as one cohesive unit within the Ahoada Division (later Rivers State).

Colonial/Post-Colonial Politics: Following the 1976 boundary adjustments (Mamman Nasir Boundary Commission), 3 villages (Mgbede, Aggah, and Okwuzi) were moved to Rivers State, while the remaining 13 remained in Imo State (Ohaji/Egbema).

Current State: The clan is currently split between two states (Imo and Rivers), with the traditional stool still having oversight over the entire area despite the political division.

Eldest And Igwaji Dichotomy

A key feature of Egbema traditional structure is the hierarchy based on age and land ownership:

The Eldest Communities: Mgbede and Okwuzi are considered the traditional elder brothers and the oldest communities founded by the descendants of the progenitor, often taking precedence in traditional matters.

Igwaji (New Yam Festival): The Igwaji Aggah is a significant annual New Yam festival that unifies all 16 communities, serving as a cultural bond that overrides the political division.

The distinction between the communities is largely based on seniority (first-born status) rather than a rigid hierarchy of power.

The Eldest: Mgbede is often considered the eldest village because of its origin from Ntia (the elder son).

The Igwaji Dichotomy: Igwaji (or Iguadiaga) is the traditional new yam festival and the supreme sociocultural unifier of the Egbema people. The Igwaji dichotomy refers to the staggered or unique nature of the celebration across the villages, particularly acknowledging the seniority of the pioneering villages (Mgbede/Okwuzi) in performing certain rituals first. Despite the political division, the Igwaji festival remains a key cultural event that reinforces the unity of the Egbema people across both states, with the eldest communities often playing a central role in the festivities.

Institutions Before And After Nze-Obi

Before Nze-Obi: Originally, Egbema society was highly democratic and decentralized, with autonomous villages managed by traditional leaders called Ezeali (custodians of the land) and elders.

Emergence of Nze-Obi: To strengthen their voice during the regional administrative changes, the community centralized its leadership.

The Throne: The Nze-Obi of Egbema was established as the paramount ruler for the entire Egbema ethnic nationality. Nzeobi Sunny N. A. Uzor of Abacheke was a major figure in this role.

Post-Emergence: The Nze-Obi (similar to 'The Sage' or Supreme Leader) functions as the centralized leader, working with individual Ezealis to manage both the Imo and Rivers sectors of Egbema. The political institution of Egbema underwent a transition from decentralized, communal leadership to a more centralized system.

1. Before Nze-Obi (Pre-colonial/Early Colonial Era):
Ezeali: Each of the 16 villages functioned independently under their local monarch, the Ezeali (King of the Land).
Decision Making: Decisions were made by a council of elders and the Ndi Ichie (title holders) within each community. There was no single king for the entire Egbema nation.

2. After Nze-Obi (Mid-20th Century to Present):
The Rise of Nze-Obi: To provide a unified front for colonial negotiation and later in the struggle for development, the office of the Nze-Obi of Egbema was established, creating a paramount ruler (Sovereign) for all Egbema communities.
HRH Eze S.N.A Uzor: The most prominent early holder of this office was HRM Eze S.N.A Uzor, who reigned over the 16 villages before the 1976 division.

The Politics of Division:
On February 3, 1976, the Egbema Kingdom was "forcefully sliced" by the Murtala Muhammed regime, creating a lingering political division.
Imo State Side: 13 villages, including the seat of the Nze-Obi, fell into Ohaji/Egbema LGA, Imo State.
Rivers State Side: 3 major communities (Mgbede, Aggah, and Okwuzi) were moved to Ogba/Ndoni/Egbema LGA, Rivers State.

Current Status: Despite the state-level divide, the Nze-Obi is still regarded as the symbolic leader of the Egbema nation, and the people maintain their cultural unity through shared festivals and bloodlines.

Present Day Time

Today, Egbema is a vital oil-producing region, rich in natural resources, but faces development challenges due to its political splitting between two states. The community continues to operate under the traditional leadership of the Nze-Obi while seeking unity and a consolidated local government administration, often advocating for a return to a single, unified administrative unit.

Traditional Structure And Functions

Umu-Okpara (Elder Sons)

In Egbema, the Umu-okpara (eldest sons of a lineage/kindred) hold the highest authority in family matters. Their function is to manage family land, act as custodians of ancestral shrines, and adjudicate disputes within the kindred.

Umelenye (Elders/Lineage Heads)

These are the keepers of tradition and custodians of the Ofo (symbol of authority) in the villages. They determine land sharing and maintain peace among families.

Umu-ada (Daughters of the Lineage)

The Umuada are a powerful, organized group of daughters born into a kindred (whether married inside or outside the community). They hold significant power, often acting as the final authority in domestic disputes, managing burials, and enforcing moral codes, particularly concerning sacrilege (Nso Ala).

Eze-nwadiala

This title refers to a king or ruler who is often related to the community through a female line (a "son of the daughter"). They act as a mediating force, bridging different families or communities.

Age-Grade and Wrestling

Formation: Age-grades, known as Ogbo, are formed by men born within a specific 3-5 year range.

Wrestling as a Selection Tool: Wrestling competitions (Igba-Mgba) are crucial for testing strength, bravery, and maturity. These competitions help transition boys into adulthood, allowing them to join the "youth" age-grade (Otu-Udu) to defend the community, and eventually transition to the "elder" age-grade responsible for governance.

Land Sharing (Farmlands)

Traditional Method: Farmlands are traditionally shared annually by the Umelenye and Umu-okpara based on the principle of communal ownership, where families are allocated plots based on size and need for the farming season.

Modern Equivalent: Today, this traditional yearly rotation is increasingly replaced by long-term leases, cash crops farming (like palm plantation), and direct land acquisitions for oil operations, causing significant, long-term impact on subsistence farming.

Information Dissemination (Town-Criers)

Town-Criers (Onye-oziza): Information is transmitted using a town-crier. He uses a local gong (Ogele) to attract attention at designated central locations before delivering messages from the Eze or council of elders.

Justice and Oaths

Oaths and Sacrilege: Cases of stealing, adultery, or murder are often handled through oaths (Iru-Alu) to the deity, such as the earth goddess (Ala). An accused person might be asked to swear before a local deity, with the belief that the deity will punish the guilty party within a specific timeframe.

Adultery Law: Adultery is considered Nso-Ala (sacrilege/abomination). The Umuada or elders often punish this by fining the man involved heavily and forcing the woman to apologize, sometimes leading to banishment or a spiritual cleansing of the community. If a wife commits adultery and the husband was told that his wife is having affairs outside and the man keep doubting or tried to defend his wife in such act, and the same man kept eating what his wife cook, the man will fall sick at first, if the man failed to stop eating from his wife the gods of the land will strike the man dead. Then if the man admit his wife's atrocities he will not die.

Marital Processes and Divorce

Marriage: The process involves several steps: Iju-ese (inquiring about the girl), Iku-aka (formal proposal), and the traditional marriage itself, which includes payment of dowry to the family and specific items for the Umuada, family heads, and youth.

Divorce: Divorce is uncommon and viewed as a last resort. It is typically handled by the Umuada of the woman's family and the elders of the man's family, who look into the cause and decide on the return of the dowry if the woman is at fault, or allowed to leave peacefully if it's the man's fault.

Modern Era And Oil Exploration

  • Oil Discovery: Egbema is a major oil-producing area with significant oil and gas deposits.
  • Marginalization and Conflict: Despite being a major oil producer, the region reports marginalization, lack of infrastructure, and ecological devastation (air, water, land pollution).
  • Administration: The Egbema King (Nze Obi of Egbema) traditionally oversees his subjects in both Imo and Rivers States.

Location And Cultural Roots

Egbema spans Imo and Rivers States, sharing a unified ancestry despite being split by political boundaries in 1976. They are primarily a sub-dialect of the Igbo language (Igboid) group located in the Niger Delta hinterland, with a history influenced by early interactions with European traders. They are distinct from the Ijaw Egbema in Delta/Edo states.

Origins And Ancestral Roots

  • Location: In Imo State (Ohaji/Egbema LGA) and Rivers State (Ogba/Egbema/Ndoni LGA)
  • Ethnicity: Closely linked to Ogba, Egi, and Ndoni groups.
  • Trade and Economy: Traditionally involved in farming and fishing, navigating rivers and creeks.
  • Language: A sub-dialect of Igbo, with strong linguistic links.

Environmental And Socio-economic Challenges

Oil exploitation has brought significant environmental degradation and socioeconomic issues, including pollution and marginalization despite the region's rich resources.

Egbema Oil kingdom spans the rich flood plains of Imo and Rivers States, a major oil-producing region with deep ancestral roots. Our 16 autonomous communities maintain traditional governance system (Nze Obi) while collectively fostering development and cultural preservation.

Since the discovery of commercial oil in the late 1956s, Egbema has contributed over 5% of Nigeria's petroleum output. Yet our identity remains tied to the land, water, and festivals that celebrates our ancestors. We are stewards of both black Gold and living heritage.

Cultures & Traditions

Masquerade festivals, sacred water spirits, and the rhythm of the talking drum defines Egbema identity.

From the annual Alinso Festival that celebrates ancestral reverence to vibrant wrestling contests showcasing young warriors, our culture pulsates with life. Traditional marriage rites, folk music, and the iconic Egbema-Igbo cuisine (spicy fish pepper soup, palm wine) creates a rich tapestry.

Egbema Masquerade (Ekpo/Mmawu/Okroshi/Owu)

Honoring ancestors, the masquerades appear during dry and even rainy seasons- messengers between the living and the spirit world. The community gathers to witness dances, moral parables, and blessings. Each masquerade has a unique role: Ekpo, Mmawu, Okroshi, and Owu.

The Oil Economy And Modern Impact

Discovery: Commercial oil exploration began in the late 1950s, profoundly changing the economy from traditional Agriculture.

Production: The area is home to over forty producing oil wells operated by multinationals like Shell, Agip, and Total Energy.

Impact: While oil has boosted national revenue, the community has faced challenges related to environmental degradation and the need for infrastructure development.

Geopolitical Dissection

It is important to distinguish between two main "Egbema" entities:

1. Ijaw Egbema (Edo/Delta): Traditionally located along the Benin River in Edo State, governed by the Agedagba.

2. Igbo Egbema (Imo/Rivers): Located within the Ogba/Egbema/Ndoni local government Area of Rivers and Ohaji/Egbema local government Area of Imo. The Egbema people continue to be part of the Niger Delta's and by extension, Nigeria's economic history.

Egbema (Igbo Riverine Clan)

Riverine Igbo clan located in the lower Niger flood plains across Imo and Rivers States, comprising 16 traditional communities (Villages) known for oil and gas production. The major communities often under Nze Obi Leader include Mmahu, Abacheke, Obiakpu, Etekwuru, Obokofia, Ofuruola, Umuorji, Ekugba, Mgbara, Umudike, Obeakpu, Opuoma, Abaezi, Okwuzi, Mgbede, and Aggah. The communities are situated in a region with high oil production and agriculture activity, with some parts falling under Ohaji/Egbema LGA of Imo State and others in Ogba/Egbema/Ndoni LGA of Rivers State.

Egbema Alinso: Also known as Ohaji/Egbema, established in 1976 in Imo State, is an oil-rich region formed from the old Owerri and Ogboko divisions, predominantly inhabited by the Igbo ethnic group, known as a major economic hub. It is historically characterized by agricultural productivity (Adapalm) and massive oil/gas resources, though often cited as politically marginalized.

Key Historic Aspects

Establishment & Geography: The local government was created in 1976. The Egbema Clan was split during this period with some villages (Okwuzi, Mgbede, Aggah) becoming part of Rivers State while the majority remained in Imo State.

Economic Significance: It is a major contributor to Nigeria's economy, housing major oil and gas installations such as the Egbema west plant station, Ebocha plant station, Obiakpu Location plant station, Assa North- Ohaji South project and the Mmahu Power plant in 2006.

Cultural Background

Originally known as "Alinso" (Sacred Land), Egbema is traditionally governed by the Nze Obi, while Ohaji has its own distinct cultural structure.

Administrative & Development Issues

Despite its wealth, the region has historically felt underdeveloped, leading to advocacy for better infrastructure and a greater share of the 13% derivation funds for host communities managed by ISOPADEC and RISOPADEC. The area remains a critical, yet sometimes neglected, economic hub.
